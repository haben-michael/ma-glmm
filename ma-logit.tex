\title{Bartlett adjustment for random effects meta-analysis of event outcomes with small samples}
% \date{}
\author[1]{Haben Michael}
\affil[1]{University of Massachusetts, Amherst, MA (hmichael@math.umass.edu)}
\date{}                   
\begin{document}
\maketitle

\textsc{Abstract.} \citet{stijnen:10} describes four random effects models for
meta-analyses of event outcomes. Inference for these models is based
on an asymptotic chi-squared approximation. We describe a procedure
for Bartlett correction of the confidence interval, improving the
convergence rate from $1/\n$ to $1/\n^2$ under ideal conditions. We
apply the proposed procedure to meta-analyses considered by \citet{stijnen:10} on anti-infection devices, finding a differences in CI lengths
up to 10\%.

\textsc{Keywords: Meta-analysis, Bartlett correction,
  Generalized linear mixed models}


\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

There is currently underway a move to favor generalized linear mixed
models in carrying out meta-analyses of event outcomes, particularly
when sample sizes are small or events are rare. GLMMs avoid many of
the dubious assumptions and devices, such as perfect knowledge of the
study standard deviations and zero-cell corrections, required by the
traditional alternative, inverse-variance weighting after a normalizing transformation. % have been since stijnen
% 2010, glmm have become the preferred model for random effects
% meta-analysis based on count data. cochran etc have recommended ... .
However, inferences under GLMMs are still based on an asymptotic
chi-squared approximation. Therefore, as in the case of
inverse-variance weighted estimation, the conclusions of smaller
meta-analyses are less trustworthy, though smaller  meta-analyses are where the use
of GLMMs is often recommended. A classical remedy is Bartlett
correction, which leverages modeling assumptions to improve the
efficiency of the testing procedure. When the modeling assumptions
are met, the improvement is typically a jump in the rate of asymptotic
convergence from $1/\n$ to $1/\n^2$. 

While Bartlett corrections have long been applied to generalized
linear models \citep{cordeiro:83}, there do not appear to be
extensions to even the simple GLMMs that are used in
meta-analysis. The reason for the reluctance appears to
be computational difficulty \citep{hanada:26}. While computer algebra
systems have long been applied to compute the third- and fourth-order
derivative arrays needed for Bartlett correction of GLMs
\citep{cordeiro:93,mccullagh:87,andrews:00}, closed-form expressions
for GLMM Bartlett corrections are unavailable for any of the commonly
used models other than the normal-normal
\citep{noma:11,michael:ma-bartlett}. A combination of automatic
symbolic manipulation and numerical approximation appears
necessary. % Unlike in the case of GLMs, closed-form expressions are
% unavailable for Bartlett corrections for GLMMs.[[check there are closed forms for glms!! ]]
We argue that with
proper design these difficulties can be overcome and consumer-grade
hardware is sufficient for practical implementation.

In the next section we present four event data meta-analysis models,
following \citet{stijnen:10}'s exposition. We then summarize the method of
Bartlett correction, and discuss some of the underlying
assumptions. In Section \ref{sec:method} we offer a practical approach
to estimation of the Bartlett correction applicable to the four
models described in Section \ref{sec:background}. In Section
\ref{sec:simulation} we examine by simulation the effectiveness and robustness of
the corrected test, finding that the proposed approximations are suitable for achieving the desired rate boost and identifying common model violations that may neutralize the advantage. In Section \ref{sec:data-analysis} we
re-visit the data analyses of \citet{stijnen:10}. We find that, though the
advantage of Bartlett correction may be  negligible for 
meta-analyses of moderate or large size, it may be clinically relevant in smaller meta-analyses.

\subsection{Statistical models}

We consider the random effects model for a meta-analysis consisting of $\n$ studies. % in all casea a random effect models variation between the effects of
% the studies. this random effect is modelled as gaussian
In this model, a Gaussian random variable models the effects $\re_1,\ldots,\re_n,$ of the individual studies in the meta-analysis,
\begin{align}
  \re_i = \munull + \taunull\Z_i,\qquad \Z_i \sim N(0,1)
\end{align}
The target of inference in the meta-analysis is $\munull$, with
$\tausqnull$ a nuisance parameter. The model for the conditional distribution of the
data given the random effect depends on the type of
analysis being conducted. Following \citet{stijnen:10} we consider four types of analyses, determined by whether the studies included are single-arm or two-arm trials, and whether the estimand is based on proportions or incidence.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Single arm, proportions. Each study reports the number of events $\Y_i$ and total number of trials $\m_i$.  The statistical model is binomial
  \begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
      f_{\m_i}(\y_i\mid \Z_i) &=  {\m_i \choose \y_i}p^{\y_i}(1-p)^{\m_i-\y_i}\\
  p = p(\munull,\tausqnull) &= \expit(\munull + \taunull\Z_i)
\end{split}
\label{mod:bn-prop}
\end{equation}
The target of inference $\munull$ represents the log-odds  of an event. The number of trials $\m_i$ is treated as a parameter of the conditional PMF $f_{m_i}$, which therefore typically varies across the studies in the meta-analysis.

\item Two arms, proportions. Each study reports, for treatment and control arm, the number of events $\T{\Y}_i$ and $\C{\Y}_i$ and the total number of trials $\T{\m}_i$ and $\C{\m}_i$. The model is conditional on the total number of events $S_i=\T{\Y}_i+\C{\Y}_i$, implying $\T{\Y}_i$ follows Fisher's non-central hypergeometric distribution
  \begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
      f_{\T{\m}_i,\C{\m}_i,S_i}(\T{\y}_i \mid \Z_i) &= \frac{{\T{\m}_i\choose \T{\y}_i}{\C{\m}_i\choose S_i-\T{\y}_i}\omega^{\T{\y}_i}}
  {\sum_{\max(0,S_i-\C{\m}_i)}^{\min(S_i,\T{\m}_i)}
                               {\T{\m}_i\choose \y}{\C{\m}_i\choose S_i-\y}\omega^{ \y}}\\
  \omega = \omega(\munull,\tausqnull) &= \exp(\munull + \taunull\Z_i)
\end{split}
\label{mod:hn}
\end{equation}
The target of inference $\munull$ represents the ratio of the treatment to control log-odds. The parameters of the conditional PMF for study $i$ are $\T{\m}_i, \C{\m}_i,$ and $S_i$.

\item Single arm, incidence rate. Each study reports the number
of events $\Y_i$ and the duration of observation $T_i$.  The model is
Poisson,
  \begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
  f_{T_i}(\y_i\mid \Z_i) &= \exp(-\lambda)\frac{\lambda^{\y_i}}{\y_i!}\\
  \lambda = \lambda(\munull,\tausqnull) &= T_i\exp(\munull + \taunull\Z_i)
\end{split}
\label{mod:pn}
\end{equation}
The target of
inference $\munull$ represents the log of the incidence rate. The follow-up time $T_i$ is treated as a parameter of the conditional PMF.

\item Two arms, incidence rates. Each study reports, for treatment and control arm, the number of events $\T{\Y}_i$ and $\C{\Y}_i$ and the duration of observation $\T{T}_i$ and $\C{T}_i$. Conditionally on $S_i=\T{\Y}_i+\C{\Y}_i$, $\T{\Y}_i$ is binomial,
  \begin{equation}
    \begin{split}
  f_{\T{T}_i,\C{T}_i,S_i   }(\T{\y}_i \mid \Z_i) &= {S_i \choose \T{\y}_i}p^{\T{\y}_i}(1-p)^{S_i - \T{\y}_i}\\
  p &= p(\munull,\tausqnull) = \expit(\log(\T{T}_i/\C{T}_i)+\munull + \taunull\Z_i)
\end{split}
\label{mod:bn-irr}
\end{equation}
The target of inference is the logarithm of the ratio of the incidence
rates between the two arms. The sum of events $S_i$ and the
periods $\T{T}_i$ and $\C{T}_i$ are teated as parameters of the
conditional PMF.
\end{enumerate}
In all cases the likelihood for  study $i$ has the form
\begin{align}
  L_i(\effect,\tau^2 | \Y_i) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f_i(\Y_i|\z)\phi(\z)d\z, \qquad i=1,\ldots n
  \label{defn:L-unit}
\end{align}
where $f_i(y|\z)$ is study $i$'s  PMF conditional on a Gaussian random effect. Furthermore, the meta-analysis  parameters $(\effect,\tau^2)$ only enter the likelihood \eqref{defn:L-unit} through a parameter of the conditional PMF, namely, the mean $\lambda$ in the case of the Poisson, the success probability $p$ in the cases of the binomial, or the odds ratio $\omega$ in the case of the non-central hypergeometric.
% int condpmf * phi. condpmf = , parameters= (used loosely--need to distinguish from mu/tau2).

The likelihood ratio (``LR'') test of the null $\effect=\munull$ is based on the test statistic
\begin{align}
  % \W = 2\sum_{i=1}^n(\log L_i(\hat\effect,\hat\tau^2 | \Y_i) - \log L_i(\munull,\hat{\tau}^2_0 | \Y_i) )\\
  \W &= 2( l(\hat\effect,\hat\tau^2) - l(\munull,\hat{\tau}^2_0) )
    \label{defn:W}
\end{align}
where
\begin{align}
  l(\effect,\tau^2) &= \sum_{i=1}^n\log L_i(\effect,\tau^2 | \Y_i)
    \label{defn:l}
\end{align}
is the total log-likelihood, $(\hat\effect,\hat\tau^2)$ maximizes
% $(\effect,\tau^2)\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^n\log L_i(\effect,\tau^2 | \Y_i)$
$(\effect,\tau^2)\mapsto l(\effect,\tau^2)$,
and $\hat{\tau}^2_0=\hat{\tau}^2_0(\munull)$ maximizes
% $\tau^2 \mapsto\sum_{i=1}^n\log L_i(\munull,\tau^2 | \Y_i)$
$\tau^2 \mapsto l(\munull,\tau^2)$.  In routine applications  $\W$ converges to a chi-squared
distribution  under the null hypothesis, by Wilks' Theorem. Relying on this asymptotic approximation a confidence interval
for $\munull$ can be formed by inverting the test statistic; see Figure \ref{fig:data-analysis:profile-LR} for an example.


\subsection{Bartlett correction}

Under a range of conditions the mean of a correctly specified LR statistic \eqref{defn:W} admits an expansion in integral powers of $1/\n$ \citep{barndorff:84}
\begin{align}
  \E(\W)  = 1 + b/\n + O(1/\n^2).
\end{align}
Therefore, the Bartlett-corrected statistic
\begin{align}
  \WB = \frac{\W}{1+b/\n}
  \label{defn:WB}
\end{align}
has a mean agreeing with that of a chi-squared random variable up to order $O(1/\n^2)$.
% write out expansion of likelihood. odd powers of
% root n vanish. identify c as the 1/n term. so dividing by 1+c then
% removes the mean bias to order 1/n,

The results of \citet{bartlett:37,lawley:56} show further that, under
appropriate conditions, all other moments, and so also the entire
distribution, also agree with a chi-squared up to order
$O(1/\n^2)$. This rate improvement holds even when  MLEs are plugged in for any unknown parameters appearing in the  bias term $b$ of the expansion \citep{barndorff:88}.

% well, i.e., the chi squared approximation.

% random variable is $O(1/\n^2)$.
As with the LR statistic, the
adjusted statistic may be inverted to obtain CIs and
p-values; see Figure \ref{fig:data-analysis:profile-LR} for an example. % [mention b depends on parameters, or maybe make explicit in
% the notation, and that 1/n2 rate can be achieved when these are estimated by root-n consistent estiamtros such as the mle's.]]

\citet{lawley:56} provides a formula for the correction $b$ appearing
in \eqref{defn:WB} as sums and products of the components of the
inverse Fisher information for \eqref{defn:l} and certain tensors $\kterm$. Being a small set
of sums and products this formula does not introduce numerical or
computational difficulties that aren't already present in the basic
tensor components input into the formula, so we focus here on the
tensor components. To this end, let $D_r$ denote partial differentiation with
respect to a sequence of the parameters $\effect$ and $\tau^2$ given
by $r$. For example,
$D_{\mu,\tau^2}\sum_{i=1}^n\log L_i(\effect,\tau^2\mid \Y_i)$ denotes
the off-diagonal term of the observed information matrix for \eqref{defn:l}. Define
$\k_r=\k_r^{(0)}=\E D_r \sum_{i=1}^n\log L_i(\effect,\tau^2\mid \Y_i)$
and $\k_r^{(s)} = D_s \k_r$. In this notation, for example, the Fisher
information matrix has components
$\k_{\mu,\mu}, \k_{\mu,\tau^2}, \k_{\tau^2,\mu},
\k_{\tau^2,\tau^2}$. Lawley's formula for the Bartlett correction
requires all terms $\k_r$ where $r$ is of length 2, 3, or 4, and all
terms $\k_r^{(s)}$ where $r$ is of length $3$ and $s$ of length $1$ or
$r$ is of length $2$ and $s$ of length $1$ or $2$. Taking into account the commutativity of differentiation,
% permutation symmetry of the order of differentiation
 there are $35$
such terms in a model with one parameter of interest and one nuisance
parameter.

By linearity of expectation and differentiation, it suffices to
compute the terms $\k_r^{(s)}$ for the unit log-likelihoods
$\log L_i(\effect,\tau^2\mid \Y_i), i=1,\ldots,\n$. 
Let us consider a single study from the meta-analysis, dropping the
subscript $i$ from the conditional PMFs and likelihoods. Then the general tensor component needed for the computation of Bartlett's correction is
\begin{align}
  k_r^{(s)} 
  &=  \sum_y D_s\left( \left(D_r \log\int f(y|\z)\phi(\z)d\z\right)  \int \w_k f(y|\z)\phi(\z)d\z\right)
    \label{defn:k-term-exact}
\end{align}
The outer sum is taken over the support of $\Y$.

\section{Computing the tensor components $\kterm$}
\label{sec:method}


\subsection{Overview}
\label{sec:method-overview}

To compute the tensor component \eqref{defn:k-term-exact}, we approximate the integrals
by a quadrature rule. Since all the likelihoods \eqref{mod:bn-prop}--\eqref{mod:bn-irr} involve
integrating against a Gaussian, Gauss-Hermite (``GH'') quadrature is a natural
choice. This and similar methods \citep{liu:94} provide
weights $\w_1,\ldots,\w_{\nGH}$ and nodes $\z_1,\ldots,\z_{\nGH}$ in
the domain of integration allowing the Gaussian integral of smooth
integrands such as appear in \eqref{defn:L-unit} to be approximated by a weighted
sum,
\begin{align}
    k_r^{(s)} 
  &\approx  \sum_y D_s\left( \left(D_r \log\sum_{j} \w_j  f(y|\z_j)\right)  \sum_k \w_k f(y|\z_k)\right)
    \label{defn:k-term-approximate}
\end{align}
The weights and nodes of the quadrature may in general depend on the
unknown parameters $\effect$ and $\tau^2$. To simplify the above
derivatives we assume the weights and nodes are constant with respect to the
parameters, as discussed further in Section \ref{sec:method:tuning-parameters}. The number of nodes $\nGH$
controls the quality of the approximation and introduces a tuning
parameter into the proposed method.  As in \eqref{defn:k-term-exact},
the outer sum is taken over the support of $\Y$, which is a finite set
in all the models considered here other than the Poisson-normal model \eqref{mod:pn}. The
Poisson model requires a truncation point for computation, which
introduces a further tuning parameter.

Carrying out the differentiations in \eqref{defn:k-term-approximate} yields
rational expressions in derivatives of the weighted sums
$\sum_{j} \w_j f(\y|\z_j)$, say,
$\F_{kl}(\y)=\sum_{j} \w_j \frac{d^k}{d\mu^k}\frac{d^l}{d(\tau^2)^l}
f(y|\z_j)$. These 35 expressions for $\kterm$ in terms of the $\F_{kl}$ are
the same for any model based on a likelihood of the form \eqref{defn:L-unit}  and may be pre-computed. For example,
$\k_{\mu,\mu}=
\sum_\y(\F_{20}(\y)-(\F_{10}(\y)^2/\F_{00}(\y))$), % and $\k_{\mu,\tau^2}^{(\tau^2)}=(\F_{01}^2\F_{10})/\F_{00}^2 - (\F_{02}\F_{10} + \F_{01}\F_{11})/
% \F_{00} + \F_{12}$,
and $\k_{\mu,\tau^2}^{(\tau^2)}=\sum_\y(\F_{01}(\y)^2\F_{10}(\y)/\F_{00}(\y)^2 - (\F_{02}(\y)\F_{10}(\y) + \F_{01}(\y)\F_{11}(\y))/
\F_{00}(\y) + \F_{12}(\y))$.

The derivatives $\F_{jk}(\y)$ of the model-specific conditional PMFs
that appear in the 35 tensor components $\kterm$ number 15: $\F_{jk}(\y)$ for
$0\le k+l \le 4$. The form of these derivatives may be derived
independently of data but their specific values in general depend on
parameters of the conditional PMF, e.g., the trial sizes $m_i$ in the binomial-normal model \eqref{mod:bn-prop}, and must typically be
computed for each study of a meta-analysis. % The latter computations
% may be carried out in parallel when the meta-analysis consists of
% stochatically independent studies, such as we consider
% here.
However, since the conditional PMFs only depend on the model
parameters $(\effect,\tau^2)$ through the PMF's distributional
parameters, the derivatives $\F_{jk}(\y), 0\le k+l \le 4$, may be
re-written as linear combinations  of $\F_{00}(\y)=\sum_{j} \w_j f(y|\theta_j)$ using standard
recurrences \citep{johnson:05}. The advantage of this form is that the terms
$\F_{00}(\y)$ involve only the PMF itself, for which optimized and
numerically stable routines are available, unlike the derivatives of the PMF. For example, two
applications of the Poisson PMF recurrence
$ \frac{d}{d\lambda} f_{\lambda}(\y\mid \Z) = f_{\lambda}(\y-1\mid \Z)
- f_{\lambda}(\y\mid \Z), \y\ge 1, $ yield, for the Poisson-normal model \eqref{mod:pn},
\begin{align}
  \F_{11}(\y) &= \sum_j\w_j \frac{d^2}{d\mu d\tau^2} f_{\lambda(\effect,\tau^2)}(\y\mid \z_j)\\
              &= \frac{d\lambda}{d\mu} \frac{d\lambda}{d\tau^2}\F_{00}(\y-2)
                +( \frac{d^2\lambda}{d\mu d\tau^2} - 2 \frac{d\lambda}{d\mu} \frac{d\lambda}{d\tau^2}) \F_{00}(\y-1)
                + \frac{d\lambda}{d\mu} \frac{d\lambda}{d\tau^2} \F_{00}(\y),\qquad \y\ge 2
\end{align}

The approach in brief is then, for each of the $\n$ studies in the
meta-analysis, 1. evaluate the 15 vectors
$\F_{jk}(\y), \y\in \operatorname{supp}(\Y)$, with $\operatorname{supp}(\Y)$ truncated in the case of Poisson $\Y$,  using only off-the-shelf routines for
the conditional PMF (Poisson, binomial, or hypergeometric) and basic
arithmetic, 2. compute the 35 $\kterm$ terms using only the computed 15
$\F_{jk}(\y)$ vectors and basic arithmetic. The 15 $\F_{jk}(\y)$ terms can be
computed in parallel, and once they are computed the 35 $\kterm$ terms
can be computed in parallel. As the studies are independent the entire
computation of the study-level Bartlett correction can be parallelized
across the $\n$ studies, as well.


\subsection{Tuning parameters}
\label{sec:method:tuning-parameters}

We discuss the main choices made in applying the proposed method to a
given dataset: the number of nodes to be used in the quadrature rule,
whether the nodes should be chosen using a fixed or adaptive rule,
and, in the case of the Poisson-normal model, the truncation point for
the support.

\begin{enumerate}


\item The main tuning parameter of the proposed method is $\nGH$, the
  number of nodes in the quadrature approximation. We propose selecting
  this parameter heuristically. In the proposed method there are 15
  integrands to which quadrature is applied, i.e., the functions $z\mapsto \frac{d^k}{d\mu^k}\frac{d^l}{d(\tau^2)^l}
  f(y|\z)\phi(z)$, $0\le k+l \le 4$, which show up as the summands in
  $\F_{kl}(\y)=\sum_{j} \w_j \frac{d^k}{d\mu^k}\frac{d^l}{d(\tau^2)^l}
  f(y|\z_j)$. We therefore examine plots of the 15
  integrands, weighted by the quadrature weights in place of the
  normal density, versus quadrature nodes. For a safe choice of $\nGH$ the plotted values should be negligible outside the range of $z_1,\ldots,z_{\nGH}$. See Figure \ref{fig:data-analysis:diagnostic-pmf} for an
  example. Typical choices encountered in the datasets below, leading
  to apparent convergence up to $.001$ absolute error in the CI
  endpoints, are 100--200 nodes for fixed rule quadrature and 10--30
  for adaptive rule quadrature.

\item Two broad variants of GH quadrature are fixed and
  adaptive. Fixed GH uses the same $\nGH$  nodes centered at $0$
  irrespective of the integrand, whereas adaptive GH
  transforms the nodes to take into account the mode and scale of the
  integrand. The drawback of adaptive quadrature is the cost of
  repeated numerical estimation of the mode and scale of the
  integrand of \eqref{defn:L-unit}. For example, computing  MLEs
  under models \eqref{mod:bn-prop}--\eqref{mod:bn-irr} by Newton-Raphson requires evaluating the
  integrals in \eqref{defn:l} at a sequence of values $(\effect,\tau^2)$, and
  under adaptive quadrature each such evaluation requires estimating
  the mode and scale of a new integrand, leading to nested
  optimizations. This cost is partially mitigated by the fewer nodes
  generally required by adaptive quadrature.

  A further difficulty with adaptive quadrature is conceptual. Since
  the adaptive nodes and weights depend on the parameters
  $(\effect,\tau^2)$ through the mode and scale of the integrand, they are subject to
  the differentiation operators in \eqref{defn:k-term-approximate}, which is incompatible with the
  assumptions of the proposed method. Ignoring this dependence
  involves a tacit assumption that the mode and scale of the derivatives of the integrand are similar to the integrand's.  However, this
  simplified approach leads to efficient implementation of adaptive
  GH. The only type of integrand for which a mode must then be computed is
  of the form \eqref{defn:L-unit}. As all the conditional
  PMFs belong to the exponential family, these integrands are strictly
  log-concave and unimodal, and the mode and scale may be obtained
  quickly using Newton-Raphson.

  In most of the data analysis examples in Section
  \ref{sec:data-analysis}, there is in fact little difference in performance
  between CIs returned by fixed and adaptive quadrature, the exception being the Poisson-normal analysis; see Figure \ref{fig:data-analysis-diagnostic-k}.

  implementation of the proposed method requires a truncation

\section{Simulation}
\label{sec:simulation}

We examine by simulation the rate at which the LR and
Bartlett-corrected LR statistics converge to the reference chi-squared
distribution under the four models
\eqref{mod:bn-prop}--\eqref{mod:bn-irr}. The purpose is three-fold:
1. to investigate whether the quadrature approximations used by the
proposed method are sufficient, 2. to investigate whether the Bartlett
correction is effective as applied to this type of lattice data, as
there are few theoretical results here, and 3. to assess the
robustness of any improvement in the rate offered by Bartlett
correction to deviations from the assumed non-degenerate Gaussian
model for the random effect. Results are given in Table
\ref{tab:simulation} and Figure \ref{fig:simulation:tau2}.

\subsection{Design}
To make the simulation results relevant, the simulated meta-analyses
are constructed to resemble the meta-analysis datasets of \citet{niel:07} and \citet{niel:08}  analyzed by
\citet{stijnen:10} and re-analyzed in Section
\ref{sec:data-analysis}% , reproduced in Tables
% \ref{tab:niel-weise-07} and \ref{tab:niel-weise-08}
. The true effect $\munull$ and heterogeneity $\tausqnull$ are taken
to be the MLEs for the data obtained by \citet{stijnen:10}, and the
distributional parameters are obtained by sampling with replacement
from the datasets. For example, to simulate a meta-analysis of a
single proportion of size $\n$ resembling the treatment arm data of \citet{niel:07},
we first sample with replacement $\n$ trial sizes $m_1,\ldots,m_{\n},$
from the $18$ treatment arm study sizes given by the corresponding
column of Table \ref{tab:niel-weise-07}; and second, sample $\n$
effects $\Y$ under the binomial likelihood \eqref{mod:bn-prop} using
the sampled trial sizes $m_i$ and random success probabilities sampled using the parameters $(\munull,\tausqnull)=(-3.50,.81)$ taken from the
MLE calculations of \citet{stijnen:10}. Meta-analyses of sizes
$10,\ldots,20,$  are sampled in this manner, reflecting the sizes $\n=9$ and $\n=18$ of \citet{niel:07} and \citet{niel:08}. The
rate of convergence of the empirical Kolmogorov-Smirnov distance to a
chi-squared distribution is then estimated using $\n^4$ monte carlo
repetitions at each sample size $\n$. See \citet{michael:ma-bartlett}
for details on the rate estimation.

Robustness of the Gaussianity assumption is examined by perturbing a
normal to a skewed distribution \citep{jones:09}. The skew
was chosen to test robustness since it drives the first-order error in
CLT-type approximations. Robustness against non-degeneracy of the
random effect, $\tausqnull>0$, is studied in particular cases
below.% the sinh-asinch model was used to obtain the
% perturbned gaussian distributions.

In all simulations, we have used the true parameters
$(\munull,\tausqnull)$, perturbed by a Gaussian with standard deviation
$1/\sqrt\n$, to calculate the Bartlett correction
$b=b(\munull,\tausqnull)$. In practice the MLE would be used. The practical
reason is to avoid the prohibitive computational cost of
re-calculating the Bartlett calculation for each of the $\n^4$ monte
carlo replicates. The justification is the result of \citet{lawley:56,barndorff:88} and others that the $O(1/\n^2)$ Bartlett rate survives substitution of $\sqrt\n-$consistent estimators into the Bartlett correction $b(\mu,\tau^2)$.
% correction term $b(\mu,\tau^2)$
% plugging $\sqrt\n-$consistent estimators into the Bartlett
% correction term $b(\mu,\tau^2)$, itself $O(1/\n)$, only introduces an $O(1/\n^2)$ error.



\subsection{Results}

Table \ref{tab:simulation} shows that when the model assumptions are
met, the standard LR rate of $1/\n$ and the Bartlett-corrected rate of
$1/\n^2$ are approximated by three of the four models \eqref{mod:bn-prop}--\eqref{mod:bn-irr}. As
skew increases, the rate deteriorates for both
statistics. It appears also that skew affects the Bartlett-corrected statistic more severely than the standard statistic, with the corrected statistic in some instances showing a slower rate when skew is high. We therefore conclude that, at least in these three
models and under correct specification of the random effect, the quadrature approximations may be sufficient.%  and that skew
% may affect the convergence of both corrected and uncorrected LR
% statistics.  

The binomial-normal model for incidence rate ratios model
\eqref{mod:bn-irr} is the exception, showing a rate closer to
$1/\n^{3/2}$ than $1/\n^2$ under Bartlett correction even in the
idealized skew-free setting. This failure appears to be due to low
heterogeneity. In addition to  the MLE
$\hat\tau^2=0.096$ of the original data set \citep{niel:08}, we consider a smaller and larger choice of
$\tau^2$, maintaining other aspects of the simulation design. The
results, presented in Fig. \ref{fig:simulation:tau2}, show the rate
deteriorating as $\tau^2 $ is decreased and improving to the target $1/\n^2$ rate as
$\tau^2$ grows. The standard LR statistic does not appear much affected,
consistent with previously reported simulations
\citep{jackson:18,bakbergenuly:18}. We therefore conclude that the
advantage of Bartlett correction can be impaired by low heterogeneity.

\section{Conclusion}
\label{sec:conclusion}

We have presented a method to more accurately compute confidence
intervals for meta-analyses of event outcomes. Discreteness of the data does not appear to be an obstacle to achieving fast rates. We have seen that the improvement offered by the
proposed method deteriorates as the model assumption of a
non-degenerate Gaussian random effect is progressively relaxed.

Ideally application of the Bartlett correction would be a ``free
lunch,'' offering an improved rate if the random effect is in fact Gaussian and otherwise being neutral, beyond perhaps the added computational cost. Since a Bartlett correction is only of order $O(1/n)$ whether misspecified or not, such a desire may not be unreasonable. %  if the assumptions like Gaussianity of the random effect are
% met, then we get the benefit of the rate improvement, and if they're
% not met, our inferences are the same as using the uncorrected
% statistic.
We have seen this is not quite the case, however. When the skew of the random
effect is sufficiently far from $0$, the rate of the
Bartlett-corrected statistic can be worse than the uncorrected
statistic. Presumably a random effect sufficiently far from a Gaussian
in other respects can also lead to this reversal.
