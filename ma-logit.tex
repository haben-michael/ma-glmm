\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

There is currently underway a move toward generalized mixed models in
carrying out meta-analyses of event outcomes, particularly in
situations where sample sizes are small or events are rare. GLMMs
avoid many of the dubious assumptions, such as gaussianity of the effects and
perfect knowledge of standard deviations, required by the traditional alternative,
inverse-variance weighting. % have been since stijnen
% 2010, glmm have become the preferred model for random effects
% meta-analysis based on count data. cochran etc have recommended ... .
However, inferences under GLMMs are still based on an asymptotic
chi-squared approximation. Therefore, as in the case of
inverse-variance weighted estimation, the conclusions of smaller
meta-analyses are suspect, though smaller  meta-analyses are where the use
of GLMMs is often recommended. A classical remedy is Bartlett
correction, which leverages modelling assumptions to improve the
efficiency of the testing procedure. When the modelling assumptions
are met, the improvement is typically a jump in the rate of asymptotic
convergence from $1/\n$ to $1/\n^2$. [[the key modelling assumption in
this setting is the gaussianity of the random effect.]]

While Bartlett corrections have long been applied to generalized
linear models [[cite]], there do not appear to be extensions to even
the simple GLMMs that are used in meta-analysis. The reason for the
reluctance appears to be computational difficulty [[cite
hanada]]. Unlike in the case of GLMs, closed-form expressions are
unavailable for Bartlett corrections for GLMMs.[[check there are closed forms for glms!! ]]  We argue that with
careful design these difficulties can be overcome and consumer-grade
hardware is sufficient for practical implementation.

In the next section we summarize 4 event data meta-analysis models,
following [[Stifjnen's]] exposition. In Section ((ref)) we describe a
practical approach to estimation of the Bartlett correction
applicfable to these 4 models. In Section ((ref)) we examine the
effectiveness and robustness of the corrected test, finding ... . In Section ((ref))
we re-visit the data analyses of ((stijnen)), finding ... .

We consider the random effects model for a meta-analysis consisting of $\n$ studies. % in all casea a random effect models variation between the effects of
% the studies. this random effect is modelled as gaussian
In this model, a Gaussian random variable models the effects $\re_1,\ldots,\re_n,$ of the individual studies in the meta-analysis,
\begin{align}
  \re_i = \munull + \taunull\Z,\qquad \Z \sim N(0,1)
\end{align}
The target of inference in the meta-analysis is $\munull$, with
$\tausqnull$ a nuisance parameter. The conditional distribution of the
data given the random effect depends on the type of
analysis being conducted. Following ((stijnen)) we consider 4 types of analyses, determined by whether the studies included are single-arm or two-arm trials, and whether the estimand of the meta-analysis is a proportion or incidence rate.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Single arm, proportions. Each study reports the number of events $\Y_i$ and total number of trials $\m_i$.  The statistical model is binomial
\begin{align}
  % \Y_i\mid \theta_i \sim binomial(\m_i,\expit(\theta_i))
  % L(\theta,\tausqnull
  % f(\y_i\mid \Z) &=  {\m_i \choose \y_i}(\expit(\re_i))^{\y_i}(1-\expit(\re_i))^{\m_i-\y_i}\\
  % &=  {\m_i \choose \y_i}\expit( \munull + \taunull\Z)^{\m_i}(1-\expit( \munull + \taunull\Z))^{\m_i-\y_i}
      f_{\m_i}(\y_i\mid \Z) &=  {\m_i \choose \y_i}p^{\y_i}(1-p)^{\m_i-\y_i}\\
  p = p(\munull,\tausqnull) &= \expit(\munull + \taunull\Z_i)
\end{align}
The target of inference $\munull$ represents the log-odds  of an event. The number of trials $\m_i$ is treated as a parameter of the conditional PMF $f_{m_i}$, which therefore typically varies across the studies in the meta-analysis.[[replace f with fi in displays, update next 3 similarly]]

\item Two arms, proportions. Each study reports, for treatment and control arm, the number of events $\T{\Y}_i$ and $\C{\Y}_i$ and the total number of trials $\T{\m}_i$ and $\C{\m}_i$. The statistical model is conditional on the total number of events $S_i=\T{\Y}_i+\C{\Y}_i$, under which $\T{\Y}_i$ follows Fisher's non-central hypergeometric distribution
\begin{align}
  % f(\T{\y}_i \mid \Z_i, S_i) &= \frac{{\T{\m}_i\choose \T{\y}_i}{\C{\m}_i\choose S_i-\T{\y}_i}\exp(\re_i\T{\y}_i)}
  % {\sum_{\max(0,S_i-\C{\m}_i)}^{\min(S_i,\T{\m}_i)}
  % {\T{\m}_i\choose \y}{\C{\m}_i\choose S_i-\y}\exp(\re_i \y)}\\
  f_{\T{\m}_i,\C{\m}_i,S_i}(\T{\y}_i \mid \Z_i) &= \frac{{\T{\m}_i\choose \T{\y}_i}{\C{\m}_i\choose S_i-\T{\y}_i}\omega^{\T{\y}_i}}
  {\sum_{\max(0,S_i-\C{\m}_i)}^{\min(S_i,\T{\m}_i)}
                               {\T{\m}_i\choose \y}{\C{\m}_i\choose S_i-\y}\omega^{ \y}}\\
  \omega = \omega(\munull,\tausqnull) &= \exp(\munull + \taunull\Z_i)
\end{align}
The target of inference $\munull$ represents the ratio of the treatment to control log-odds. The parameters of the conditional PMF are $\T{\m}_i, \C{\m}_i,$ and $S_i$.[[ref to bivriate model which models baseline risk, mentions assumptions in this approach]]

\item Single arm, incidence rate. Each study reports the number
of events $\Y_i$ and the duration of observation $T_i$.  The model is
Poisson,
\begin{align}
  f_{T_i}(\y_i\mid \Z_i) &= \exp(-\lambda)\frac{\lambda^{\y_i}}{\y_i!}\\
  \lambda = \lambda(\munull,\tausqnull) &= T_i\exp(\munull + \taunull\Z_i)
\end{align}
The target of
inference $\munull$ represents the log of the incidence rate. The follow-up time $T_i$ is treated as a parameter of the conditional PMF.

\item Two arms, incidence rates. Each study reports, for treatment and control arm, the number of events $\T{\Y}_i$ and $\C{\Y}_i$ and the duration of observation $\T{T}_i$ and $\C{T}_i$. Conditionally on $S_i=\T{\Y}_i+\C{\Y}_i$, $\T{\Y}_i$ is binomial
\begin{align}
  f_{\T{T}_i,\C{T}_i,S_i   }(\T{\y}_i \mid \Z_i) &= {S_i \choose \T{\y}_i}p^{\T{\y}_i}(1-p)^{S_i - \T{\y}_i}\\
  p &= p(\munull,\tausqnull) = \expit(\log(\T{T}_i/\C{T}_i)+\munull + \taunull\Z_i)
\end{align}
The target of inference is the logarithm of the ratio of the incidence
rates between the two arms. The sum of events $S_i$ and and the
periods $\T{T}_i$ and $\C{T}_i$ are teated as parameters of the
conditional PMF.
\end{enumerate}
In all cases the likelihood for  study $i$ has the form:
\begin{align}
  L_i(\effect,\tau^2 | \Y_i) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f_i(\Y_i|\z)\phi(\z)d\z, \qquad i=1,\ldots n
\end{align}
where $f_i(y|\z)$ is study $i$'s  PMF conditional on a Gaussian random effect, as given above.
% int condpmf * phi. condpmf = , parameters= (used loosely--need to distinguish from mu/tau2).

The likelihood ratio test of the null $\effect=\munull$ is based on the test statistic
\begin{align}
  \W = 2\sum_{i=1}^n(\log L_i(\hat\effect,\hat\tau^2 | \Y_i) - \log L_i(\munull,\hat{\tau}^2_0 | \Y_i) )
\end{align}
where $(\hat\effect,\hat\tau^2)$ maximizes
$(\effect,\tau^2)\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^n\log L_i(\effect,\tau^2 | \Y_i)$
and $\hat{\tau}^2_0=\hat{\tau}^2_0(\effect)$ maximizes
$\tau^2 \mapsto\sum_{i=1}^n\log L_i(\munull,\tau^2 | \Y_i)$.  In routine applications  $\W$ converges to a chi-squared
distribution  under the null hypothesis, by Wilks' Theorem. Relying on this asymptotic approximation a confidence interval
for $\munull$ can be formed by inverting the test statistic.

\end{align}
Therefore, the Bartlett-corrected statistic
\begin{align}
\WB = \frac{\W}{1+b/\n}
\end{align}
has a mean agreeing with that of a chi-squared random variable up to order $O(1/\n^2)$.

\section{Method}
\label{sec:method}

lawley ((cite)) provides a formula for the correction ((ref)) as sums
and products of the components of the inverse Fisher information for
((ref likelihood)) and certain tensors [[k terms]]. Being a small set
of sums and products this formula does not introduce numerical or
computational difficulties that aren't already present in the basic
tensor components, and we omit it [[is not of direct interest
here]]. Let $D_r$ denote partial differentiation with respect to a
sequence of the parameters $\effect$ and $\tau^2$ given by $r$. For
example, $D_{\mu,\tau^2}\sum_{i=1}^n\log L_i(\effect,\tau^2\mid \Y_i)$
denotes the off-diagonal term of the obseerved information matrix for
((ref total likelihood)). Define $\k_r=\E D_r l(\effect,\tau^2)$ and
$\k_r^{(s)} = D_s \k_r$. In this notation the Fisher information
matrix has components
$\k_{\mu,\mu}, \k_{\mu,\tau^2}, \k_{\tau^2,\tau^2}$. Lawley's formula
for the Bartlett correction requires all terms $\k_r$ where $r$
is of length 2, 3, or 4, and all terms $\k_r^{(s)}$ where $r$ is of
length $3$ and $s$ of length $1$ or $r$ is of length $2$ and $s$ of
length $1$ or $2$. Taking into account permutation symmetry of the
order of differentiation, there are $35$ such terms in a model with one parameter of interest and one nuisance paramter.

Since the meta-analysis data is typically non-identically distributed, the tensor components ((k)) must be computed separately for each study.We consider a single study and drop the subscript $i$ from the conditional PMFs and tensor components ((k)) for ease of exposition.[[also above k referrd to the sum. need to say we are using linearity to compute k's for each study]]. To compute the tensor components ((ref)) for the likelihood ((ref)),
we approximate the integral by a quadrature rule. Since all the
likelihoods ((ref)) involve integrating against a Gaussian,
Gauss-Hermite quadrature is a natural choice, and for the sake of
simplicity we adopt here a non-adaptive variant. This and similar
methods consist in weights $\w_1,\ldots,\w_{\nGH}$ and nodes in the
domain of integration $\z_1,\ldots,\z_{\nGH}$ allowing the integral
of smooth integrands like ((ref)) to be approximated by a weighted sum, 
\begin{align}
 L(\re,\tau^2 | \Y) =  \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(\Y|\z)\phi(\z)d\z
  \approx  \sum_{j}\w_j  f(\Y|\z_j)
\end{align}
The number of nodes $\nGH$ controls the quality of the approximation and introduces a tuning parameter into the proposed method. A higher-order partial derivative of the log-likelihood may be approximated by
   $D_r \log L(\effect,\tau^2 | \Y) \approx  D_r \log\sum_{j} \w_j  f(\Y|\z_j)$. The expectation of the latter  may be approximated by another quadrature
\begin{align}
  \k_r &=  \sum_\y  \left(D_r \log L(\effect,\tau^2 | \y)\right)  \int f(y|\z)\phi(\z)d\z\\
  &\approx  \sum_\y  \left(D_r \log L(\effect,\tau^2 | \y)\right) \sum_k \w_k f(\y|\z_k)\\
       &\approx  \sum_\y  \left(D_r \log  \sum_j \w_j f(\y|\z_j)\right)  \sum_k \w_k f(\y|\z_k)
\end{align}
The outer sum is taken over the support of $\Y$, which is a finite set in all the models considered here other than the Poisson model. The Poisson model requires a truncation rule, an additional tuning parameter discussed further below.
% expression ((...)) approximates k terms of the form ((inner only)). k terms of the form ((inner and outer)) may be approximated by 
The general tensor component needed for the computation of the Bartlett correction factor is then approximated as
\begin{align}
    k_r^{(s)} 
      &\approx  \sum_y D_s\left( \left(D_r \log\sum_{j} \w_j  f(y|\z_j)\right)  \sum_k \w_k f(y|\z_k)\right)
\end{align}

Carrying the differentiations ((ref above display)) through yields rational expressions in
derivatives of the weighted sums $\sum_{j} \w_j f(\y|\z_j)$, say, $\F_{kl}(\y)=\sum_{j} \w_j \frac{d^k}{d\mu^k}\frac{d^l}{d(\tau^2)^l}  f(y|\z_j)$. The
   35 tensor components ((k)), in terms of the  $\F_{kl}$, are the same for any
model based on a likelihood of the form ((ref)), and may be
pre-computed. For example $\k_{\mu,\mu}=  \sum_\y(\F_{20}(\y)-(\F_{10}(\y)^2/\F_{00}(\y))$), % and $\k_{\mu,\tau^2}^{(\tau^2)}=(\F_{01}^2\F_{10})/\F_{00}^2 - (\F_{02}\F_{10} + \F_{01}\F_{11})/
% \F_{00} + \F_{12}$,
and $\k_{\mu,\tau^2}^{(\tau^2)}=\sum_\y(\F_{01}(\y)^2\F_{10}(\y)/\F_{00}(\y)^2 - (\F_{02}(\y)\F_{10}(\y) + \F_{01}(\y)\F_{11}(\y))/
\F_{00}(\y) + \F_{12}(\y))$.

The derivatives $\F_{jk}(\y)$ of the model-specific conditional PMFs
that appear in the 35 tensor components number 15: $\F_{jk}(\y)$ for
$0\le j+k \le 4$. The form of these derivatives may be derived
independently of data but their specific values in general depend on
parameters of the conditional PMF ((see above)), and must typically be
computed for each study of a meta-analysis. The latter computations
may be carried out in parallel when the meta-analysis consists of
stochatically independent studies, such as we consider here. Furthermore, using standard recurrences ((cite johnson and kotz univariate discrete
distributions)) the derivatives $\F_{jk}(\y), 0\le j+k \le 4$, may be
re-written  as polynomial expressions $\F_{00}(\y)=\sum_{j} \w_j f(y|\theta_j)$, involving only the PMF itself, for which optimized and numerically
stable routines are available. For example, two applications of the
Poisson PMF recurrence
$ \frac{d}{d\lambda} f_{\lambda}(\y\mid \Z) = f_{\lambda}(\y-1\mid \Z)
- f_{\lambda}(\y\mid \Z), \y\ge 1, $ yield, for model ((ref
poisson/incidence rate model)),
\begin{align}
  \F_{11}(\y) &= \sum_j\w_j \frac{d^2}{d\mu d\tau^2} f_{\lambda}(\y\mid \z_j)\\
              &= \frac{d\lambda}{d\mu} \frac{d\lambda}{d\tau^2}\F_{00}(\y-2)
                +( \frac{d^2\lambda}{d\mu d\tau^2} - 2 \frac{d\lambda}{d\mu} \frac{d\lambda}{d\tau^2}) \F_{00}(\y-1)
                + \frac{d\lambda}{d\mu} \frac{d\lambda}{d\tau^2} \F_{00}(\y),\qquad \y\ge 2
\end{align}
This device exploits the fact that the model parameters $(\effect,\tau^2)$ only enter the likelihood ((ref)) through a parameter of the conditional PMF, the mean $\lambda$ in the case of the Poisson, the success probability $p$ in the cases of the binomial, or the odds ratio $\omega$ in the case of the non-central hypergeometric.[[maybe in 4 definitions make explicit dependence of param on mu,tau2 ]]
