\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}

There is currently underway a move toward generalized mixed models in carrying out meta-analyses of event outcomes, particularly in situations where sample sizes are small or events are rare. GLMMs avoid many of the dubious assumptions of the main competing approach, inverse-variance weighting, such as gaussianity of the effects and perfect knowledge of standard deviations. % have been since stijnen
% 2010, glmm have become the preferred model for random effects
% meta-analysis based on count data. cochran etc have recommended ... .
However, inferences under GLMMs are still based on an asymptotic
chi-squared approximation. Therefore, as in the case of
inverse-variance weighted estimation, the conclusions of smaller
meta-analyses are suspect, while smaller meta-analyses are where use
of GLMMs is often recommended. A classical remedy is Bartlett
correction, which leverages modelling assumptions to improve the
efficiency of the testing procedure. When the modelling assumptions
are met, the imrpovement is typically a jump in the rate of asymptotic
convergence from $1/\n$ to $1/\n^2$. [[the key modelling assumption in
this setting is the gaussianity of the random effect.]]

While Bartlett corrections have long been applied to generalized
linear models [[cite]], there do not appear to be extensions to even
the simple GLMMs that are used in meta-analysis. The reason for the
reluctance appears to be computational difficulty [[cite
hanada]]. Unlike in the case of GLMs, closed-form expressions are
unavailable for Bartlett corrections for GLMMs.  We argue that with
careful design these difficulties can be overcome and consumer-grade
hardware is sufficient for practical implementation.

In the next section we summarize 4 event data meta-analysis models,
following [[Stifjnen's]] exposition. In Section ((ref)) we describe a
practical approach to estimation of the Bartlett correction
applicfable to these 4 models. In Section ((ref)) we examine the
effectiveness and robustness of the corrected test, finding ... . In Section ((ref))
we re-visit the data analyses of ((stijnen)), finding ... .

We consider the random effects model for meta-analysis. % in all casea a random effect models variation between the effects of

In this model, a Gaussian random effects models the effect of the individual studies,
\begin{align}
  \re_i = \munull + \taunull\Z,\qquad \Z \sim N(0,1)
\end{align}
The target of inference in the meta-analysis is $\munull$, with
$\tausqnull$ a nuisance parameter. The conditional distribution of the
data given the random effect depends on the type of
analysis being conducted. Following ((stijnen)) we consider 4 types of analyses, determined by whether the studies included are single-arm or two-arm trials, and whether the estimand of the meta-analysis is a proportion or incidence rate.
\begin{enumerate}
\item Single arm, proportions. Each study reports the number of events $\Y_i$ and total number of trials $\m_i$.  The statistical model is binomial
\begin{align}
  % \Y_i\mid \theta_i \sim binomial(\m_i,\expit(\theta_i))
  % L(\theta,\tausqnull
  % f(\y_i\mid \Z) &=  {\m_i \choose \y_i}(\expit(\re_i))^{\y_i}(1-\expit(\re_i))^{\m_i-\y_i}\\
  % &=  {\m_i \choose \y_i}\expit( \munull + \taunull\Z)^{\m_i}(1-\expit( \munull + \taunull\Z))^{\m_i-\y_i}
      f(\y_i\mid \Z) &=  {\m_i \choose \y_i}p^{\y_i}(1-p)^{\m_i-\y_i}\\
  p &= \expit(\munull + \taunull\Z_i)
\end{align}
The target of inference $\munull$ represents the log-odds  of an event. The number of trials $\m_i$ is treated as a parameter of the conditional PMF $f_i=f_{m_i}$, which therefore depends on the study in the meta-analysis.[[replace f with fi in displays, update next 3 similarly]]

\item Two arms, proportions. Each study reports, for treatment and control arm, the number of events $\T{\Y}_i$ and $\C{\Y}_i$ and the total number of trials $\T{\m}_i$ and $\C{\m}_i$. The statistical model conditions on the total number of events $S_i=\T{\Y}_i+\C{\Y}_i$, under which $\T{\Y}_i$ follows Fisher's non-central hypergeometric distribution
\begin{align}
  % f(\T{\y}_i \mid \Z_i, S_i) &= \frac{{\T{\m}_i\choose \T{\y}_i}{\C{\m}_i\choose S_i-\T{\y}_i}\exp(\re_i\T{\y}_i)}
  % {\sum_{\max(0,S_i-\C{\m}_i)}^{\min(S_i,\T{\m}_i)}
  % {\T{\m}_i\choose \y}{\C{\m}_i\choose S_i-\y}\exp(\re_i \y)}\\
  f(\T{\y}_i \mid \Z_i) &= \frac{{\T{\m}_i\choose \T{\y}_i}{\C{\m}_i\choose S_i-\T{\y}_i}\omega^{\T{\y}_i}}
  {\sum_{\max(0,S_i-\C{\m}_i)}^{\min(S_i,\T{\m}_i)}
                               {\T{\m}_i\choose \y}{\C{\m}_i\choose S_i-\y}\omega^{ \y}}\\
  \omega &= \exp(\munull + \taunull\Z_i)
\end{align}
The target of inference $\munull$ represents the ratio of the treatment to control log-odds. The parameters of the conditional PMF include $S, \T{\m}, \C{\m}$.[[ref to bivriate model which models baseline risk, mentions assumptions in this approach]]

\item Single arm, incidence rate. Each study reports the number
of events $\Y_i$ and the duration of observation $T_i$.  The model is
Poisson,
\begin{align}
  f(\y_i\mid \Z_i) &= \exp(-\lambda)\frac{\lambda^{\y_i}}{\y_i!}\\
  \lambda &= T_i\exp(\munull + \taunull\Z_i)
\end{align}
The target of
inference represents the log of the incidence rate. The follow-up time $T_i$ is treated as non-random.

\item Two arms, incidence rates. Each study reports, for treatment and control arm, the number of events $\T{\Y}_i$ and $\C{\Y}_i$ and the duration of observation $\T{T}_i$ and $\C{T}_i$. Conditionally on $S_i=\T{\Y}_i+\C{\Y}_i$, $\T{\Y}_i$ is binomial
\begin{align}
  f(\T{\y}_i \mid \Z_i) &= {S_i \choose \T{\y}_i}p^{\T{\y}_i}(1-p)^{S_i - \T{\y}_i}\\
  p &= \expit(\log(\T{T}_i/\C{T}_i)+\munull + \taunull\Z_i)
\end{align}
The target of inference
is the logarithm of the ratio of the incidence rates between the two arms. As before the periods $\T{T}_i$ and $\C{T}_i$ are teated as  parameters of the conditional PMF.
\end{enumerate}

In all cases the likelihood for  study $i$ has the form:
\begin{align}
  L_i(\effect,\tau^2 | \Y_i) = \int_{-\infty}^\infty f_i(\Y_i|\z)\phi(\z)d\z
\end{align}
where $f_i(y|\z)$ is study $i$'s  PMF conditional on a Gaussian random effect, as given above.
% int condpmf * phi. condpmf = , parameters= (used loosely--need to distinguish from mu/tau2).

The likelihood ratio test of the null $\effect=\munull$ is based on the test statistic
\begin{align}
  \W = 2\sum_{i=1}^n(\log L_i(\hat\effect,\hat\tau^2 | \Y_i) - \log L_i(\munull,\hat{\tau}^2_0 | \Y_i) )
\end{align}[[subscript on i to transmit dependence of pmf f on i]]
where $(\hat\effect,\hat\tau^2)$ maximizes
$(\effect,\tau^2)\mapsto\sum_{i=1}^n\log L_i(\effect,\tau^2 | \Y_i)$
and $\hat{\tau}^2_0=\hat{\tau}^2_0(\effect)$ maximizes
$\tau^2 \mapsto\sum_{i=1}^n\log L_i(\munull,\tau^2 | \Y_i)$.  In routine applications  $\W$ converges to a chi squared
distribution  under the null hypothesis, by Wilks' Theorem. Relying on this asymptotic approximation a confidence interval
for $\munull$ can be formed by inverting the test statistic.

lawley ((cite)) provides expressions for the correction ((ref)) as
sums and products of the components of the inverse Fisher information
and certain tensors [[k terms]]. the specific formulas arent of interest here; being a small set of sums and products they don't introduce numerical or computational difficulties that aren't already to be found in the basic k terms. Let $D_r$
denote partial differentiation with respect to a sequence of the
parameters $\effect$ and $\tau^2$ given by $r$. For example, ... denotes the off-diagonal term of the obseerved information matrix for ((ref total likelihood)) Define
$\k_r=\E D_r l(\effect,\tau^2)$ and $\k_r^{(s)} = D_s \k_r$. In this
notation the Fisher information matrix has components
$\k_{11}, \k_{12}, \k_{22}$.[[intro component notation or use mu,tau2

all terms $\k_r$ where $r$ is of length 2, 3, or 4, and all terms
$\k_r^{(s)}$ where $r$ is of length $3$ and $s$ of length $1$ or $r$ is
of length $2$ and $s$ of length $1$ or $2$. Taking into account
permutation symmetry of the order of differentiation, there are $35$
such terms.

To compute the tensor components ((ref)) for the likelihood ((ref)),
we approximate the integral by a quadrature rule. Since all the
likelihoods ((ref)) involve integrating against a Gaussian,
Gauss-Hermite quadrature is a natural choice. This and similar methods  consist in a weights and nodes in the domain of integration, the size of which controls the quality of approximation, allowing the
integral ((ref)) to be approximated by a weighted sum on, say ((how much detail to give here,[[set notation nGH and w. this tuning parameter will be needed later. say we are using fixed rule not adatpve]].

\begin{align}
 L(\re,\tau^2 | \Y) =  \int_{-\infty}^\infty f(\Y|\z)\phi(\z)d\z
  \approx  \sum_{j}\w_j  f(\Y|\z_j)
\end{align}
A higher-order partial derivative of the log-likelihood may be approximated by
   $D_r \log L(\effect,\tau^2 | \Y) \approx  D_r \log\sum_{j} \w_j  f(\Y|\z_j)$. The expectation of the latter  may be approximated by another quadrature
\begin{align}
  \k_r &=  \sum_\y  \left(D_r \log L(\effect,\tau^2 | \y)\right)  \int f(y|\z)\phi(\z)d\z\\
  &\approx  \sum_\y  \left(D_r \log L(\effect,\tau^2 | \y)\right) \sum_k \w_k f(\y|\z_k)\\
       &\approx  \sum_\y  \left(D_r \log  \sum_j \w_j f(\y|\z_j)\right)  \sum_k \w_k f(\y|\z_k)
\end{align}
The outer sum is taken over the support of $\Y$, which is a finite set in all the models considered here other than the Poisson model. The Poisson model requires a truncation rule, discussed further below.

\end{align}
